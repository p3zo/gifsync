Bars, beats and marks

Gifsync counts in beats, not bars. In 4/4 a one-bar loop is four beats, so a GIF you want to fill one bar gets four marked frames; two bars gets eight. The marks are what decide it — the number in the box is only the starting suggestion.

Pick a beat count that divides the GIF's frame count. A 24-frame GIF is happy at 4, 6, 8 or 12 beats. At 5 it has to hold some beats for five frames and others for four, and that 20% swing in speed is visible on a loop you are going to stare at. The frame timing page has the arithmetic.

Bounce the audio on a bar line

The video starts at the first beat you mark on the waveform, not at zero, and runs to the end of the audio. So bounce a section that begins exactly on a downbeat, drop it in, and put the first mark at 0.00s — then the animation's first marked frame is on your downbeat and every later one lands on a beat too.

If the bounce starts with a bar of silence or a pickup, mark the first real downbeat instead and the video will start there.

Half time is usually the answer

A GIF drawn to loop about once a second does not have to hit every beat of a 128 BPM track. Setting it to hit every two beats halves the apparent speed without slowing the animation down — the frames are simply spread over twice as much song. The examples page has the same loop at both, with sound.

What you get back

An MP4 with the audio muxed in, at the GIF's own pixel dimensions, with the frame timestamps written to the microsecond. Drop it straight back on a video timeline as a visualiser, or post it as it is. Nothing is uploaded on the way: the decoding, the retiming and the encoding all happen in the page.
